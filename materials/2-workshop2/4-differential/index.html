Differential Testing

We test which features differ between two groups of samples, using one method on both tables, and then look closely at how much a significant result is worth.

Exercise 1: Test the gene table with a grouping from the species table

Open notebooks/04-differential.qmd. Start by reading back the per-sample table you saved yesterday, the one with lacto_fraction and community in it. If you did not save it, rebuild it now; the slides from Module 2 have the recipe.

Part 1: Predict

  1. We will test 13,738 genes. If no gene were truly different between the two community types, how many would come out with a raw p-value below 0.05?
  2. You will test at week 18 only, which is 157 samples, 37 against 120. Would you rather have 157 samples at one visit or 471 samples across three visits? Say why before you look at anything.
  3. Of the genes that do come out significant, do you expect more to be higher in anaerobe-dominant samples or in Lactobacillus-dominant samples?

Part 2: Build

  1. Subset both the metadata and the gene counts to time_point == "week_18". Confirm you have 157 samples and that the column order of the count matrix matches the row order of the sample table. If they don’t match, DESeq2 gives you nonsense without any warning.

  2. Filter the genes. Keep a gene if it has a count of at least 10 in at least a quarter of the samples.

  3. Run the three lines:

    dds <- DESeqDataSetFromMatrix(counts, sample_table, ~ community)
dds <- DESeq(dds)
res <- results(dds, contrast = c("community", "anaerobe", "lactobacillus"))

  4. How many genes have padj < 0.05? How many of those have an absolute log2FoldChange above 1?

  5. Join 05_gene_annotation.csv so your results carry gene symbols rather than Ensembl identifiers. Sort by padj and read the top twenty names out loud to your group.

  6. Draw a volcano plot: log2FoldChange on x, -log10(padj) on y, coloured by whether the gene passed.

  7. Pick the single top gene and plot its normalised counts as a boxplot by community. The direction of that plot must match the sign of its log2FoldChange. Check that it does.

Part 3: Check the agent

  • Ask Pi how many genes were significant. Then ask it how many genes went into the test. If it can’t give you both numbers, it doesn’t know what it ran.
  • Ask it to explain the sign convention of your contrast. Then verify against the boxplot from step 7. Getting the direction backwards is the most common error in this analysis, and it regularly gets through peer review.
  • Ask it for the number of genes with pvalue < 0.05 as well as padj < 0.05. The first number should be far larger. If it is not, something is wrong.
  • Ask it to run the same test on CPM-normalised counts instead of raw counts. It will probably do it. Look at what happens to the results and work out why that is wrong.

Discussion

  • Did the direction of the effect surprise you?
  • What do the top genes have in common? You do not need to know the genes; read the names in gene_name and describe the pattern in plain words.
  • Someone will have used all 471 samples instead of 157. What is wrong with that, and how wrong is it?

Exercise 2: The same test, on the species table

This is the second half of the same notebook. You run the same three lines of code on the 220-row species table instead of the 14,226-row gene table, with a grouping that comes from the clinic rather than from either count table.

Part 1: Predict

  1. Why can you not test the species table using the community column you built yesterday? Answer in one sentence.
  2. You will test species against birth outcome. The species table has 220 rows, but most are nearly always absent. How many do you expect to survive a filter of “present in at least 10% of samples”? Of those, how many are present in every one of the 157 samples?
  3. If any species come out significant, do you expect them to be higher in the preterm group or the term group?

Part 2: Build

  1. Subset to time_point == "week_18" again. Filter the species table to features present in at least 10% of samples.

  2. Run the same three lines with design = ~ outcome and contrast = c("outcome", "preterm", "term"). How many species are significant?

  3. Look at how DESeq2 normalised the samples. Count the species with no zero in any sample, sum(rowSums(counts == 0) == 0). Then plot sizeFactors(dds) against each sample’s L. iners share, both on log scales. What are the size factors really measuring?

  4. Run it again with size factors built for a table full of zeros:

    dds <- DESeqDataSetFromMatrix(counts, sample_table, ~ outcome)
dds <- estimateSizeFactors(dds, type = "poscounts")
dds <- DESeq(dds)
res <- results(dds, contrast = c("outcome", "preterm", "term"))

    How many species are significant now? Keep this version for the rest of the exercise and for tomorrow.

  5. Join 03_microbiome_taxonomy.csv so you have readable names, and list the top ten species by padj with their fold changes, whether or not any pass. How many of the ten lean toward preterm?

  6. Plot the relative abundance of the top three species by outcome. Use proportions, not raw counts, and consider a log scale.

  7. Now run the test a second way, on the gene table, with the same ~ outcome design. Compare the two result sets:

    • how many features were tested in each
    • how many were significant in each
    • what share of the significant ones changed by more than a factor of two

    Write down what that comparison tells you about where the outcome signal lives.

Part 3: Check the agent

  • A filter on a sparse table is easy to get wrong. Ask Pi how many species it kept and recompute that number yourself from rowMeans(counts > 0).
  • Ask it whether DESeq2 is an appropriate method for microbiome counts. Read its answer critically. Researchers disagree about this, so a confident one-sided answer is a warning sign.
  • Ask Pi how DESeq2 computed the size factors for the species table, and how many species went into them. Check its answer against your count from step 3.
  • Ask it to name the top species. Then check two of them against 03_microbiome_taxonomy.csv by hand. Agents invent plausible species names.
  • Ask it to also run the test with ~ nugent >= 7 as the grouping instead of outcome. Fewer samples have a Nugent score, so the sample count drops. Make sure it tells you that it dropped samples.

Part 4: Reach (optional)

Run your species-by-outcome analysis on all 471 samples, with each participant appearing three times. Compare the number of significant species to your 157-sample result.

More samples gave you more hits. Are they real? What assumption did you break?

Discussion

  • The default size factors gave a list of species that anyone who works on bacterial vaginosis would recognise. Why is a familiar-looking list not evidence that the method was right?
  • One group found many more hits in the gene table than the species table, and another found the reverse depending on their filter. What does that say about how much of a differential analysis is decided before the test runs?
  • At week 18 the gene table shows an outcome signal and the species table, one species at a time, does not. Does that mean the bacteria do not matter for preterm birth? What other way of looking at the species table could still find a signal?
