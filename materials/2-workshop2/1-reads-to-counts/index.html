From Reads to Count Tables

How sequencing reads become a table of counts, for bacteria and for human genes, and what that table does and does not tell you.

There are no laptops this afternoon. The first ninety minutes are a lecture, and for the second ninety minutes you are on your feet, building count tables out of paper and objects.

Analog activities (90 minutes, no computers)

Run the activities in this order. Everyone stands and moves around for the first three, and each one sets up a number that Tuesday and Wednesday compute in code.

Materials

item how many used for
Opaque cloth or paper bags one per participant, 61 all four
Coloured beads, buttons or dried beans, 6 colours about 6,000 total all four
Flip chart sheets one per table of six, 10 activity 3
Thick markers two per table activity 3
Sticky dots one sheet per table activity 4
Pre-printed blank count-table grids 10 activity 3

Fill the bags before the session. Give most bags 100 pieces, but deliberately give six bags only 20 pieces and two bags 400 pieces. Nobody is told this, because activity 4 depends on it.

Mix the compositions on purpose, so the room contains:

  • about 20 bags that are 90% one colour
  • about 20 bags that are 60% one colour with three others present
  • about 20 bags that are spread evenly across five or six colours

Activity 1: you are a sample (15 min)

Each person takes a bag. Without counting precisely, look at your bag and describe your community out loud to the person next to you: which colour dominates, how many colours you can see.

Then: find the three people in the room whose bags look most like yours. Stand together as a cluster.

Ask the room:

  • How did you decide who was similar to you? What rule were you using?
  • Did anyone belong to two clusters?
  • Did the people with very few pieces have trouble finding a group?

The rule they used is a distance metric. On Wednesday afternoon it gets a name and a formula.

Activity 2: draw ten (20 min)

Without looking, draw ten pieces from your own bag and lay them out. Record on paper: how many colours did you get, and how many of each.

Now put them back, shake, and draw ten again. Record again.

Ask the room:

  • Did you get the same answer twice? Who got a colour the second time that they missed the first time?
  • Whose two draws disagreed the most? Look at their bag. Why?
  • If you drew only three pieces, what would happen?

This is sampling depth. A rare feature can be present in the tube and absent from the count table, and whether you see it depends on how many reads you took. Tuesday morning you will find the sample in our real data with 438 reads and decide what to do about it.

Activity 3: build the table (30 min)

In table groups of six. Pool your six bags. On the flip chart, build a count table by hand:

  • one row per colour, so rows are features
  • one column per person, so columns are samples
  • in each cell, the count of that colour in that person’s bag

You do not have to count every piece. Estimate, but estimate consistently.

Then, on the same sheet, write underneath each column:

  1. the column total, the number of pieces that person had
  2. the number of rows with a zero in that column
  3. the colour with the largest share of that column, as a percentage

Finally, as a group, answer in writing on the sheet:

  • Which two people look most alike? Which two look least alike?
  • One person has far fewer pieces than the others. Does that make their community different, or just less well measured?
  • If you wanted to compare person A to person B fairly, what would you do to these numbers first?

Tape the sheets to the wall. They stay up all week.

Those three numbers under each column are library size, sparsity and dominance. They are the first three things you will compute tomorrow morning, on both of our real tables.

Activity 4: the unfair comparison (15 min)

Ask the six people whose bags held only 20 pieces to come to the front, and two people whose bags held 400.

On a flip chart, write each of their richness values: how many distinct colours they found.

Ask the room:

  • The 400-piece bags found more colours. Are those communities more diverse, or were they just measured harder?
  • How could we make the comparison fair? Take suggestions and write all of them down.
  • Someone will suggest “take the same number from everyone”. Someone may suggest “use proportions instead of counts”. Both are real strategies with real costs.

These two suggestions are rarefaction and relative abundance, and the microbiome field has argued about which to use for fifteen years. On Wednesday we pick one and explain why.

Closing the session (10 min)

Leave the flip charts on the wall, point at them, and tell the room:

Tomorrow morning you work with the same kind of table, only with 220 rows of bacteria and 14,226 rows of human genes instead of six colours, and 471 columns instead of six people.
