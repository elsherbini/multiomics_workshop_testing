Distances and Ordination

This module measures how similar whole samples are to each other. You pick a distance, draw the ordination, then put a number on whatever pattern you think you see.

Exercise 1: Distances and ordination on the species table

Open notebooks/03-ordination.qmd.

Part 1: Predict

  1. Bray-Curtis uses abundances. Jaccard uses only presence and absence. Our species table is about 89% zeros. Which of the two do you expect to separate the two community types more clearly, and why?
  2. If you shuffled the outcome label randomly across all 471 samples, what R2 would PERMANOVA report? Give a number.
  3. One sample has 438 reads. Where do you expect it to sit in a Bray-Curtis ordination?

Part 2: Build

  1. Build the phyloseq object for the species table:

    library(microViz)
source("R/count_tables.R")
ps <- as_counts(table = "microbiome")

    Inspect it. How many taxa, how many samples, how many sample variables? Add the community column you built this morning if it is not already there.

  2. Run the pipeline three times, changing only the transform and the distance:

    • "identity" with "bray"
    • "binary" with "jaccard"
    • "clr" with "euclidean"

    Plot all three, coloured by community. Put them side by side.

  3. Colour your Bray-Curtis ordination by outcome instead. Then by time_point. Then by nugent.

  4. Run dist_permanova() on the Bray-Curtis distance with community, outcome, time_point and rnaseq_batch as variables. Report the R2 for each.

  5. Find your 438-read sample in the ordination. Was your Part 1 prediction right? Re-run the Bray-Curtis ordination with that sample removed and see whether anything else moves.

  6. Make a stacked bar plot of community composition with comp_barplot(tax_level = "Genus", n_taxa = 10), faceted or ordered by community. Most 16S papers open with this figure.

Part 3: Check the agent

  • Ask Pi for the R2 of outcome. Then ask it what the R2 of a random label would be. If it does not say “close to zero but not zero”, push back.
  • PERMANOVA with by = "margin" and by = "terms" give different answers when variables are correlated. Ask Pi which it used and why it matters.
  • Ask it to interpret your ordination. It will produce a paragraph of confident biological narrative about axis 1. Then ask it what axis 1 actually is. Neither it nor you can tell from the plot alone.
  • Ask it whether your three distance choices “agree”. Then look at the three plots yourself and decide if its answer matches what you see.

Discussion

  • Which of the three distances would you publish? Defend it in one sentence.
  • community explains a very large share of this table. Is that a finding?
  • Your ordination shows visit structure. Each participant appears three times. Does PERMANOVA know that, and does it matter?

Exercise 2: The identical pipeline on 14,000 genes

Use the same notebook. Change three words in your code from Exercise 1 and run it again on a table sixty times larger.

Part 1: Predict

  1. Bray-Curtis is standard for microbiome data. Would you use it on gene counts? Say why or why not before you try.
  2. community explained about 18% of the species table. What share do you expect it to explain of the gene table?
  3. The gene table was produced in two sequencing batches. What share do you expect rnaseq_batch to explain?

Part 2: Build

  1. Build the gene object, filtering as you go:

    ps_genes <- as_counts(table = "rnaseq", min_count = 10, min_samples = 100)

  2. Run the pipeline with "clr" and "euclidean", aggregating at rank = "symbol". Plot it coloured by community, then outcome, then rnaseq_batch.

  3. Run dist_permanova() with the same four variables you used on the species table. Put the two sets of R2 values side by side in one small table.

  4. Look at the batch row. Then do something about it: add rnaseq_batch to the PERMANOVA formula before outcome and see whether the outcome R2 changes.

  5. Check whether batch is confounded with anything you care about. A cross-tabulation of rnaseq_batch against outcome and against community is enough. If the groups were unbalanced across batches, what would that mean for tomorrow’s differential tests, which compare the groups one gene at a time?

  6. Also try Bray-Curtis on the gene table, just to see. Compare it to your CLR ordination and decide which you believe.

Part 3: Check the agent

  • Ask Pi to compare the species and gene R2 tables and tell you what it means. Its answer will probably say the gene table has “weaker biological signal”. That is a claim about biology drawn from an ordination. Is it justified? An ordination summarises every gene at once. Tomorrow you test them one at a time, so write down what you expect to find and check it then.
  • Ask it whether you should remove the batch effect. Then ask it to actually do it. Look closely at what it does, because “batch correction” covers several very different operations with very different consequences.
  • CLR cannot handle zeros without a pseudocount. Ask Pi what pseudocount it used and where that choice came from.

Part 4: Reach (optional)

Take the first two axes of your gene ordination and the first two of your species ordination, and plot gene axis 1 against species axis 1 for the same samples. Are they related?

This scatter plot is the simplest form of multiomics integration. Friday morning starts from it.

Discussion

  • Both tables showed a significant outcome effect of about 2% or less. Would you write that in a paper, and how would you phrase it?
  • The batch effect is as large as the biological effect. What should the people who generated this data have done differently?
  • You now have two ordinations of the same 471 samples. What would it take to combine them, and what would you hope to gain?
