Sets and Enrichment

Grouping genes into named sets lets you describe a long list of hits in one sentence. The same method helps much less on a table of 53 species.

Slides

Make slides full screen

Exercise 1: From a gene list to a sentence

Open notebooks/05-enrichment.qmd. You need yesterday’s preterm-versus-term results for the gene table, from step 7 of Module 4’s second exercise. If you no longer have them, rerun the three lines that made them.

Part 1: Predict

  1. You are about to test 50 gene sets. If none were truly enriched, how many would come out with padj < 0.05?
  2. Your preterm-versus-term gene list had 838 genes up and 860 down, which is almost perfectly balanced. Does that make you more or less willing to believe an enrichment result?
  3. Name one biological program you expect to be elevated in the people who delivered preterm. You will check this against the answer.

Part 2: Build

  1. Build the ranking vector from your outcome results: res$stat, named by gene, with missing values dropped. How many genes are in it?

  2. Get the Hallmark sets:

    hallmark <- msigdbr::msigdbr(species = "Homo sapiens", collection = "H")
sets <- split(hallmark$ensembl_gene, hallmark$gs_name)

    Check that the identifiers in sets are the same kind as the names of your ranking vector. If one is Ensembl and the other is a symbol, nothing will match, and fgsea will return almost nothing without raising an error.

  3. Run fgsea(sets, stat, minSize = 15, maxSize = 500). Sort by padj. How many sets are significant, and how many point in each direction?

  4. Plot the top twelve as a bar chart of NES, coloured by direction.

  5. Check for imbalance. What share of your ranking statistic is positive? What is its mean? Now do the same for your anaerobe-versus-Lactobacillus ranking, and run fgsea on that too. Compare the two in a small table: genes up, genes down, sets significant, sets pointing one way.

    Write one sentence saying which of the two enrichment results you trust and why.

  6. Take the leading edge of your top set. Map those Ensembl identifiers to symbols with 05_gene_annotation.csv and read the list. Plot normalised counts for two of them by outcome.

Part 3: Check the agent

  • Ask Pi to run the enrichment. Then ask how many of your genes matched into any set at all. An identifier mismatch is the most common failure here, and it fails silently with plausible-looking output.
  • Ask it to interpret the top five sets. It will write a confident paragraph about inflammation and preterm birth. Ask it which of those five sets share genes with each other. Overlapping sets are not independent findings.
  • Ask it whether your result could be an artifact of the ranking being lopsided. See whether it checks or just reassures you.
  • Ask it for the hypergeometric version using only your significant genes. Compare the two answers. Where they disagree, work out which is throwing away information.

Discussion

  • Was your Part 1 prediction of the elevated program right?
  • Two of the top sets overlap heavily. How would you report them without double counting?
  • Yesterday preterm against term gave you 1,698 genes and no clear story; today it gives you one sentence. What did enrichment add, and what did it hide?

Exercise 2: The same function, on 53 species

Same notebook. You will now use the identical fgsea() call on a table with 53 features instead of 13,738, with sets built from taxonomy instead of curation.

Part 1: Predict

  1. A gene set holds about 200 genes. A genus in our table holds between 2 and 12 species. What does that do to your ability to detect enrichment?
  2. Which genus do you expect to come out associated with preterm birth?
  3. Will testing ten genus sets need as aggressive a multiple-testing correction as testing fifty gene sets?

Part 2: Build

  1. Take your species-by-outcome DESeq2 results from yesterday, the poscounts version. Build the ranking vector from stat as before.

  2. Join 03_microbiome_taxonomy.csv so each species carries its Genus and Family.

  3. Build the sets:

    genus_sets <- split(results$feature, results$Genus)
genus_sets <- genus_sets[lengths(genus_sets) >= 2]

    How many genus sets do you have? How many species are in the largest?

  4. Run fgsea(genus_sets, species_stat, minSize = 2, maxSize = 50). Which genera are significant?

  5. Repeat with Family instead of Genus. Does the answer agree? Check whether the top family contains the top genus.

  6. Now do it the simple way. Just sort your 53 species by stat and print the table with genus labels. Would you have reached the same conclusion without any enrichment analysis at all?

  7. Write two or three sentences in your notebook answering: for which of our two tables was set-based analysis worth doing, and what feature of the table decides that?

Part 3: Check the agent

  • Ask Pi to build the genus sets. Then check that no species appears in two sets, and that the set sizes sum to no more than the number of species you ranked.
  • Species with a missing genus become a set called NA if you are careless. Check whether that happened.
  • Ask Pi whether enrichment analysis was useful on the species table. Then ask it to defend the opposite position. Compare the two answers and decide which argument is stronger.

Part 4: Reach (optional)

Invent a better set definition for the species table. Rather than taxonomy, group species by something functional: whether they are Lactobacillus or not, whether they are strict anaerobes, whether they are on a published list of BV-associated organisms.

Run fgsea with your hand-built sets. A set definition that encodes real biology can beat a taxonomic one, and designing such sets is research in its own right.

Discussion

  • The top genus is the one every clinician would have guessed, even though it does not pass. Is that reassuring, or a sign the analysis added nothing?
  • Taxonomy is a hierarchy and gene sets are a flat list. Does that difference matter for how you correct for multiple testing?
  • This is the last single-table module. Tomorrow we put the two tables together. Before you leave, write down what you think combining them could tell you that neither told you alone.
